# Driving an LLM loop to produce checkable work

What a four-day formal-modelling campaign cost, what it returned, and which of
the things it taught now bind on the sessions that follow it.

This is the last of five records a single workstream left behind. The other four
measure things; this one is the account, and it owes two things none of them do.
It carries **the lessons that could not be turned into rules**, and it carries
**the cost**: what was spent, what came back, and which parts were not worth the
money.

> **The models this record accounts for no longer exist.** They were deleted with
> the rest of the formal-methods apparatus once the campaign's lessons had been
> distilled into the `linkuistics` skills — the `SY-` and `TT-` columns, the
> runner `models/run.sh`, its controls and the claim catalogue
> `docs/specs/semantic-contract.md` at `delete-formal-models-k29`, and the `FN-`
> column `crates/grove-finish/models/` at `delete-finish-models-k30`. This
> document was kept precisely because the skills cite it for their authority: a
> rule whose evidence has been deleted is a rule nothing can falsify. So every
> model path named below is a description of where something was learned, not a
> link a reader can follow.

---

## For a reader who has never seen this repository

**Grove** is a methodology, and a small CLI that supports it, for driving long
pieces of work through a large language model. A workstream is a tree of
markdown files on disk; each file is one task; **one task is one model session,
start to finish**, and a task that turns out to be bigger than one session
decomposes into a directory of smaller ones instead of running long. The tree is
kept in version control, one commit per task, and the shape of the tree is the
only state there is — there is no status file and no phase file. A session opens
by reading its own task file and the chain of charters above it, does the work,
marks its file done, commits, and ends.

Some tasks are *reviewed*. When a session's work looks like it needs an
adversarial second reading, the session cuts two more tasks beside its own: a
**review** task, whose whole job is to produce findings and change nothing, and
an **integration** task, which applies them. That three-task sequence is called a
**review chain**, and nine of them ran here.

**The campaign** was a formal-modelling phase inside one such workstream. Its
subject was Grove's own protocols — how the task tree is edited, how a workstream
is torn down at the end without losing work if the machine dies mid-operation,
and how the two fit together across three version-control arrangements. It wrote
executable models of those protocols in two unrelated modelling languages,
**Alloy 6** and **Quint**, and checked them against a hand-written catalogue of
**obligations**: numbered sentences the protocol is supposed to satisfy. A
**control** is the deliberate sabotage that proves an obligation is being
checked — break the model on purpose, and the obligation should go red. An
obligation that stays **green** when its control fires is checking nothing.

The workstream was commissioned to justify splitting this repository's Rust code
into separate crates. **It never got there.** The person paying for it stopped
the work in two steps: first the two later phases, neither of which had run a
single session, and then the tail of the modelling phase itself — on the
judgement that what remained would return nothing generally applicable. What the
workstream actually produced was a method, and a pile of evidence about that
method. This document is the account of that trade.

**Where the evidence is.** Four documents, all under `docs/`, and every claim
below cites one of them or the source it names:

| record | what it is |
|---|---|
| [`loop-record.md`](loop-record.md) | every session — kind, place in the tree, outcome, commit — plus the raw cost figures. Generated by a script from the task tree at a pinned revision |
| [`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md) | all eighteen review and integration bodies opened, every finding counted under a rule fixed before counting began |
| [`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) | six claims the campaign made about itself, adjudicated one at a time against the model files rather than against the sessions that described them |
| [`formalism-findings.md`](formalism-findings.md) | the Alloy-versus-Quint comparison the modelling phase was really running: per-finding costs, hypotheses, and what neither tool established |

The first two are **derived**: a script reads the tree and the commit messages
and writes the document, pinned to a revision so it can be regenerated after the
task tree is deleted. The third is judgement with citations. This one is
judgement too, and it has no instrument behind it — which is why its own bar is
the citation, and why a claim that survives here only as somebody's recollection
has been dropped rather than softened.

## Part 1 — The cost account

### What it spent

**Wall clock and commits** ([`loop-record.md`](loop-record.md) §*Cost account, in
raw form*, at pinned change `wzmyysov`): **58 commits over 97 h 11 m** — four
days and a bit — with a **median gap between consecutive commits of 39 minutes**
and a longest of 17 h 38 m. The gap distribution is published rather than
averaged (15 under 30 m, 31 between 30 m and 2 h, 9 between 2 h and 8 h, 2 over
8 h) for a reason worth repeating: **a commit gap bounds a session from above and
does not measure one.** The two gaps over eight hours are a human asleep, not
nine-hour sessions, and a mean would have invented a cost nobody paid.

**Sessions.** **73 task files, 16 directories; 55 ran to completion, 17 were
abandoned, 1 was live** at that pin. Four more sessions ran after it — the two
that finished the harvest, the one that landed the methodology changes, and the
one writing this — so every session figure here is one pin behind the tree and
says so.

**Effort inside the modelling work**, which is the only place anything like
effort was recorded ([`formalism-findings.md`](formalism-findings.md) §*Cost,
counterfactual, verdict*): **21.2 hours of authoring recorded across nine of
twenty-three slices**, with the true total "materially higher and
unrecoverable". Where hours were kept per obligation they came to **0.289 h per
obligation** for component-scoped modelling and **0.373 h** for system-scoped
(§*H8 — placement is a real trade-off*) — the campaign's only cost-per-unit
figures, and they cover one of the two modelling languages.

**Time lost to the tools rather than to the problem: ≈ 4.2 hours recorded**, the
largest single item being three hours of performance cliffs, output-format
discovery, and a dead end in which every check silently ran at three steps
because nobody had noticed the initial state was unconstrained.

**Machine time is not a tool comparison, and reading it as one is wrong in both
directions at once.** Alloy's task-tree column cost 6888 s of CPU for 103
commands against Quint's 1209 s for 111; but the Alloy file is the only one in
the corpus carrying integers — tree positions and keys — and bitwidth arithmetic
dominates it, while the Quint file unrolls a tree walk on every transition.
**Run cost here tracks the data structure in the model, not the family**
(§*The runs this synthesis stands on*).

### What it returned

**The catalogue and the models.** 129 obligations across three scopes, checked as
**258 `(family, obligation)` cells** — each obligation in each of the two
languages — over roughly 30,000 lines of model and model documentation. The whole
corpus runs in one invocation: **791 commands, 256 cells complete, 2 declared
gaps, 0 empty, exit 0** (§*The runs this synthesis stands on*). The runner
asserts coverage in *both* directions — no obligation uncovered, no command
naming an obligation the manifest does not have — and fails on zero work.

**Fifteen named obligations, plus three defects in the runner itself, where a
green run was checking nothing.** Enumerated one by one in
[`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §1 rather than counted, and found
by ten different sessions. Two are worth reading twice. In one, a dead
tool — the model checker killed by a 6 MB heap limit before it could read its own
jar — was reported by the runner as **43 verified properties**. In another, an
entire scope's claims were green over a world in which the behaviour they were
about could not occur.

**Findings from review**, from [`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md): **45
findings raised across nine review chains, 43 confirmed real, 2 downgraded, none
dropped**, plus **10 findings the integrating sessions raised on their own** and
**24 from in-session reviewers** (17 actionable, 3 an unclear contract, 1 an
accepted trade-off, 3 noise). **No review came back empty** — including the four
cut latest, against the smallest subjects.

**A decision set.** The campaign's durable conclusions live as decision records
under `docs/adr/`, four of which are about *evidence* rather than about Grove:
[a shared-safety claim names the role, not the artifact](adr/a-shared-safety-claim-names-the-role-not-the-artifact.md),
[a lifecycle claim says what it is over](adr/a-lifecycle-claim-says-what-it-is-over.md),
[a closed partition is over outcomes, not states](adr/a-closed-partition-is-over-outcomes-not-states.md),
and [obligations follow context, not artifact](adr/obligations-follow-context-not-artifact.md).

**Three methodology rules**, shipped into the corpus every future session reads.
They are Part 2.

### What was not worth the money

Three things, and the third is the sharpest.

**1 · The phase it was commissioned to feed never ran.** The campaign's own
synthesis reaches this verdict about itself: *"the value delivered was
overwhelmingly specification correction rather than defect detection"* — findings
that change the specification, not findings a failing test can catch. It is
measured. Of every material finding in the experiment, **about four in five
yielded no executable test at all**; the exceptions are countable and are
enumerated (§*H7 — findings convert to tests. Falsified.*). The synthesis draws
the consequence itself: *"the phase that consumes it is documentation, not
implementation."* The documentation phase is one of the two that never
ran at all. The corrections landed in the catalogue, where they still are; the
phase that would have carried them into the repository's current-state
documentation never opened.

**2 · Twelve task files were written and never run.** The documentation phase's
five and the implementation phase's seven were scaffolded at the start and
abandoned in place ([`loop-record.md`](loop-record.md) §*Every session*). Five
more, in the tail of the modelling phase, were cut mid-run by sessions that could
name exactly what evidence was still missing, and were then abandoned unrun on
the same judgement of cost against value. That is 17 of 73 task files — **23% of
the tree was written and never executed**. Some of that is the methodology
working as designed: a task is cheap, and cutting one is how a session hands work
forward rather than doing it badly. Some of it is a plan that was too confident
at the start; **every single review chain in this workstream was created mid-run,
and none was planned** (§*The plan at the start, and the tree that grew*).

**3 · The most expensive open question is still open, permanently, and the code
it was supposed to decide is still there.** The modelling phase was asked, among
other things, whether a particular crash-safety layer in the teardown protocol is
actually forced. Two of its four questions came back `keep`, a
third came back `keep` for six of its ten rows — and the remaining
question, together with that third question's other three rows, came back
**`defer`, which is now the final answer rather than a placeholder**, because
the three tasks that would have supplied the missing evidence were abandoned
(`finish-keeps-a-cleanup-layer-it-has-not-proved-forced`, retired at
`delete-finish-transaction-k8` along with the layer it kept).
What stays standing on a deferred question is **10,366 lines, 34% of `src/`,
including 31 `unsafe` blocks, plus 6,701 lines of test** — for one operation, run
once per workstream, at the end.

**And the honest reading of that is not a failure.** The record says why the
layer stays: *"removing it was never shown to be safe, not that keeping it was
shown to be necessary"* — the asymmetry being that a wrong *keep* costs ten
thousand lines nobody reopens, while a wrong *delete* turns a fail-closed refusal
into a silent wrong state. What the campaign did establish before it stopped is
worth more than the verdict would have been: run against the one removal
candidate that actually exists, **three of the four safety claims retained to
judge it turned out to be blind to the difference they were retained to judge**.
A criterion met over a blind claim is the failure this whole document is about.
That is a result about instruments rather than about crash safety, and it
generalises; the verdict would not have.

**The one figure this account cannot give is the money.** There is no token
count, no attention measure, and no per-session cost anywhere in the record
([`loop-record.md`](loop-record.md) §*What this record does not establish*). Every
cost figure above is time, commits, lines or commands. **Anyone repeating this
should instrument spend on the first day**; it is the one column that cannot be
recovered afterwards, and the absence of it is why the phrase *not worth the
money* above is a judgement rather than a division.

## Part 2 — What binds: three rules, shipped

Grove's methodology is a corpus of markdown that the CLI embeds and provisions
into each model harness. A lesson that *binds* is one that was written into that
corpus, so the next session reads it whether or not anyone remembers this
campaign. Three were, and each is recorded in the corpus's own rule inventory
(`docs/specs/corpus-rule-ownership.md`) with a phrase-level sweep in
`tests/rule_ownership.rs` that fails the build if a rule stops being where the
inventory says it is.

### A′ · A control that has never been seen to fail is not a control

**The rule, as it ships** ([`content/references/execute.md`](../content/references/execute.md),
in the block on verifying a claim about your own codebase):

> A positive control that would find something whatever the tree held, and a
> cross-tree control whose pattern matches everywhere, read exactly the same
> whether or not the instrument works — which is the one property they were
> introduced to rule out. Watch each control come back **dirty** against a
> subject you know is wrong before you credit the clean read.

**Why it binds.** Grove already told sessions to run controls before believing a
clean search result. It never told them to check that the controls themselves can
fail — which is the same defect one level up, and it is the defect this corpus
kept finding in its own models. This one costs one command.

**The evidence.** It is the campaign's own working rule, applied twice in
anger — once against the model corpus's control script, and once by
`scripts/review-yield.py`, whose three deliberate mutations of its own
classification are published with their results
([`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md) §*Residues*). Both scripts were deleted
with the campaign's apparatus (`delete-formal-models-k29`); the published
residues are what the evidence rests on and they are still there. **The first of those three
mutations found a real hole**: a classification block covering the document root
was silently absorbing every unclassified item, so deleting a section's
classification left the forward check reading clean. An unrecognised failure
defaulting to success — the same defect the campaign had already found in its
model runner.

**It would be wrong if** controls that had never been exercised turned out to be
reliable anyway. In this corpus every control that was finally exercised had
something to say.

### B · The provenance of a measurement

**The rule, as it ships** ([`content/references/execute.md`](../content/references/execute.md),
its own section): finish every edit *then* measure; digest every subject before
and after and say which files those were; one measurement, one writer; **never
infer that a background job has finished from the return of the thing that
launched it**; confirm a re-run item by item rather than by matching totals.

**Why it binds.** It is generic to any session that runs anything and reports a
number, which is most of them. It fails quietly: nothing errors, every command
exits 0, and a number comes back describing something other than what you meant
to measure.

**The evidence** — five instances across four sessions
([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §5a). One session paid two hours
for it by editing a shell script while three runs were executing it, and model
files while a run was reading them. Another destroyed a measurement the opposite
way: two sweeps writing to one log file, the first launched in the background and
reported complete when the *launching call* returned, producing a log that said a
control had fired and then that it had not — "a reading that was not merely
incomplete but self-contradictory". A third froze five subjects with digests
either side and **threw away a run because a comment-only edit landed while it
was in flight** — "the freeze is the point of the digest, and a run whose subject
moved under it is not a measurement of the file it reports on, however harmless
the edit looks."

**It would be wrong if** measurements taken against files edited mid-run
reproduced when repeated. Two of the three were re-run; one produced the
self-contradictory log.

### C · An integration's body carries the review's handle, never its findings

**The rule, as it ships** ([`content/references/decompose.md`](../content/references/decompose.md),
on what a review writes into the task it cuts, and
[`content/references/integrate-review.md`](../content/references/integrate-review.md),
on where an integration reads findings from): the review writes only the
*pointer* to itself into the integration's task file; the integration reads the
findings from the review's own commit. The previous text's *"or the findings
verbatim"* is gone.

**Why it binds, and it is the most actionable thing the whole harvest produced.**
This is a structural defect in the loop, found by measuring the loop.
[`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md) §*A 100% survival rate is what a broken
instrument reads*: **in five of the nine chains, the session that "verified" the
review's findings had its own task file written by the reviewer.** The charter
*is* the finding list, and the `Done when` section is the finding list restated
as obligations. **An integration in that position has no structural place to
disagree — to reject a finding it would have to reject its own charter.**

**The evidence is the asymmetry, not the total.** 45 findings raised at the tree
level, 45 verified, **0 rejected**. Meanwhile the *in-session* channel, where the
reader owes the finding nothing, dropped **4 of 24 (17%)** as trade-off or noise.
Rejection happens in this workstream; it has only ever happened where the reader
was not being graded against the finding list. One in-session reviewer went
further and **falsified a finding the integrating session had raised itself**.

**It would be wrong if** the tree-level survival rate reflected reviewers who
were simply right every time. That cannot be separated from the structural
reading on this evidence — which is precisely why the repair is the cheap
structural one rather than a claim about anybody's accuracy.

**And the repair is partial, which the rule says out loud.** The finding list is
still the reviewer's, so an integration still cannot notice a finding the
reviewer never wrote. That residue is published in the same document
(§*What this record does not establish*), and it is the same recursion Part 3
describes: **the repair moves the hazard one level down rather than removing
it.**

## Part 3 — What could not bind, and why

**The generalisation is worth stating plainly rather than apologising for: a
lesson binds only when there is a session that could violate it.** Four separate
declines were taken on that ground, covering the five claims below; none of them
had such a session. Grove's methodology drives sessions that write code,
documents, decisions and models — it does not itself run model suites, so a rule
about what a model suite owes has no possible violator inside it. A rule with no possible violator is decoration in a
corpus every session has to read, and the corpus has a budget.

So the five below are written here instead. **They are not weaker than the three
that bound.** Three of them are among the most heavily evidenced claims the whole
campaign produced.

### 1 · A control earns a claim only when it is shown to kill that claim *for the reason claimed*

**The claim.** A green result is not evidence. A control that *can* turn the
claim red is not enough either — because a control can be killing the claim
through a defect somewhere else entirely, and it reads identically. What
establishes the link is the **isolating** measurement: run each part of the claim
as its own separate check over the same world, and show the others stay green.

**The evidence** ([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §1) is that the
campaign started with the weaker version of this sentence and its own corpus
forced the stronger one. Three named controls were found to be killing through
the wrong mechanism:

- one was turning its obligation red through a defect in the coverage sweep
  rather than through the behaviour it named;
- one was always firing through one half of a two-part claim and never the other,
  because the narrow world it ran in could not reach the second half at all;
- one was turning its obligation red under both the old wording and the repaired
  wording — *"which is evidence that the new operand was never exercised rather
  than that it was controlled."*

The isolating form is landed where it can be checked, as a comment beside the
obligation it was worked out on, in
`finish.qnt`: two controls "each measured green on the *other* operand over its
own module, which is what makes them isolating rather than merely red."

**It would be wrong if** controls added retroactively to long-green claims had
started confirming them. Ten sessions went looking for an empty green; **ten
found one.**

**And the limit is real, because the shape of the claim hides it.** This corpus
never ran an arm without controls, so **it cannot say what fraction of greens
were sound.** What it measured is the hit rate of *looking*, and that was 100%
across ten sessions. That is an argument for looking. It is not a measurement of
how much rot there was.

**Why it did not bind:** it is about model suites, and Grove runs none. The
one-command generalisation of it — *a control that has never been seen to fail is
not a control* — did bind, as A′.

### 2 · A narrowed world is evidence about the wide one only when the narrowing is a strict subset that still reaches the subject

**State it in that conditional form, and not as the headline the campaign
originally wrote for itself.** The original was *"false greens are found by
narrowing, not widening"*, and the second half is falsified twice
([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §3).

**What is true.** One safety claim was violated in a world that narrowed the
environment to a strict *subset* of the wide world's behaviours — so the
counterexample is the wide world's too — and the wide world's 8000 random samples
had never drawn it. That measurement is recorded in the model, not in a session's
notes.

**What is not true, and the ratio is five to one against the original wording.**
In this corpus, narrowing is the dominant way false greens were *made*. Five
modules were narrowed into worlds that could not reach the thing they were
mutating: one could never produce the overlap it was about; one could never reach
the artifact half of the claim it was killing; one asserted a claim over a world
with nothing for the claim to be about; one ran a control at a depth where the
operation it mutates never happens; and one hid a real bug from the very session
that wrote it — *"the narrowing I had just argued was safe was also what hid a bug
from me."*

**And *not widening* is falsified twice.** One dead control was repaired by
widening the bound, and fired in 1.5 seconds. And a declared impossibility — an
argument, from a state-count estimate, that a particular check could not be
run — was killed by simply running the deeper attempt: the state it claimed was
out of reach was reached in fourteen states in 8.9 seconds, and two commands
already sitting in the same file had reached both boundaries. The catalogue
gained the rule that came out of it: **a modelling language's failure to meet a
catalogue row is established by running the deeper attempt, never by costing it
in prose.**

**Both halves of the surviving conditional are load-bearing, and this corpus
violated each.** The subset property is what makes a *violation* found in a
narrow world a violation in the wide one, and it has to be established by
hand — twice here it was established after the fact. Reachability is what makes a
*green* mean anything, and five modules failed it. The corpus's own compression,
reached independently three times, is the better sentence: **a mutant module's
environment is part of its control.**

**It would be wrong if** a narrowed module had reported a violation that did not
reproduce widely. None did — but only because the subset property was checked
every time, twice by hand and after the fact.

**Why it did not bind:** same reason. Environments and mutant modules are model
apparatus.

### 3 · Run every module against every claim the model has, not the ones it declares

**The claim.** In this corpus a module that changes what the model does carries
only the checks written inside it — a deliberate rule, and a correct one, because
each such module exists precisely because some obligation behaves differently
there. **Its shadow is that a module can be green while quietly violating claims
it never mentions**, and the coverage instrument built to stop exactly that
cannot see it.

**The evidence** ([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §4), and it is
the sharpest sentence in the tree. A module declared a *witness* for an
obligation while never checking that obligation's *invariant* — thereby
**"crediting [the obligation]'s coverage cell from a world in which [it] was
false"** — the brackets standing in for the obligation's own number. The
two-direction coverage assertion, the instrument built precisely to stop a cell
being credited without evidence, passed.

**The full claim library was swept against a single module exactly twice, and
both sweeps came back with findings nobody was looking for** — four in total,
including an obligation violated with no sabotage applied at all. There has
never been a clean sweep.

**It would be wrong if** such a sweep came back with nothing beyond the module's
own declared kills. Neither of the two did.

**The cost the original claim omits, and this tree measured.** Reading a module
against every claim is cheap; **repairing what it finds may not be.** One repair
was scoped and abandoned because the parameter it needed had to be added to
thirty-nine module instantiations. **The obligation is to run the sweep and
record what it says — not to fix everything it finds in the same session.**

**Why it did not bind:** it is a rule about module systems in model checkers,
and there is no Grove session that could break it.

### 4 · A claim stated over facts its own mechanism records is self-certifying, and the repair recreates the hazard one level down

**The claim, in full, including the recursion, which is the part that matters.**
If a check is satisfied by consulting the system's own record of what it did,
then the check cannot fail, and no amount of green means anything. The natural
repair is to have each step *record the fact* as it establishes it — and that
recreates the same problem one level down, because now the flags are written by
exactly the steps the claim is about. **So every recorded fact owes a control
that can make a step omit or falsify it.**

**The evidence is one complete cycle across three sessions**
([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §2), and it is named as one
instance rather than counted as four:

1. an in-session reviewer found a claim whose second half was an enumeration of
   the two protocols that happened to exist;
2. the session repaired it by recording the fact at the step that establishes it;
3. **the next session's reviewer found the repair self-certifying** — "there is no
   independent control that can make one of those sites omit or falsify either
   flag";
4. the session after that repaired *that*, with two isolating kills.

Four independent instances of the first clause exist besides, in three different
scopes. The derived obligation is landed in a decision record, in those words:
**"A green with no available kill is not a pass"** — and every fact a step
records therefore owes a control that can make a step omit it.

**It would be wrong if** the hazard were one people avoid unprompted. Instead a
single node hit it three times, at three different places in the same claim's
expression, and a fourth time in the repair of the first three.

**Why the model-level form did not bind — and why its loop-level twin did.**
Model claims and model steps are model apparatus. But the *loop* has the same
shape, exactly: an integration verifying a finding list that the reviewer wrote
into its own charter is a claim stated over its own subject's record. **That is
rule C in Part 2** — and it carries the recursion with it, because the repair
(read the findings from the review's commit) still leaves the finding list as the
reviewer's. The corpus even shows where the missing control lives: the only
channel in this workstream that has ever rejected a finding is the in-session
one, where the reader owes the finding nothing.

### 5 · A check written by calling the definition it is about stops measuring the moment that definition is repaired

**The claim.** Not the same as B, though they share a sentence in the campaign's
own recollection. B is about a *file* moving under a run. This is about a
**definition** moving under a measurement *of that definition*: a witness written
as "…and *P*" where *P* is exactly the predicate under repair. Fix *P*, and the
witness silently stops measuring the thing it was written to measure. Nothing
errors. The number just changes.

**The evidence is exactly one instance, and this is stated plainly because the
alternative is borrowing** ([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §5b).
One witness went from reached in 3410 traces to unreached in 8000 samples the
moment its repair landed — **"not because the defect went away but because the
sentence had come to mean something else"**. The fix was to write the four
pre-repair clauses out *inline* and rename the witness accordingly, giving what
the decision record that cites it calls "the same disk under the pre-repair
encoding, frozen inline: an A/B on one world."

**Why it did not bind, and the reasoning is worth keeping** because it is a
methodology decision in miniature. [`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md)
§5 deliberately split this claim from its five-instance sibling **so that its
single instance would not borrow the sibling's weight**. Landing them side by
side in the corpus as one rule would have rejoined the sentence the adjudication
had just taken apart. It is here instead — and it is worth the space, because it
is **the cheapest failure to miss in this whole document**: no error, no red, no
signal at all, just a number that quietly starts describing something else.

**It would be wrong if** a self-calling witness kept measuring across a repair of
its own subject. In the one case observed, it did not.

## Part 4 — What the loop taught about itself

Neither rules nor lessons about models: measurements of how the workstream
actually behaved, which are the part a stranger deciding whether to run one of
these should read.

**Most of the tree was discovered, not designed.** The workstream opened with 23
task files under 5 directories. It closed with 73 under 16. **61 further entries
were created by sessions mid-flight — 68% of the final tree** — and 11 of the 16
directories were created by a session rather than by the plan
([`loop-record.md`](loop-record.md) §*The plan at the start* and §*Nodes*). This
is the methodology working: a task is a cheap unit, and a session that finds the
work bigger than its charter is supposed to split it rather than run long. It is
also the reason the plan's own estimates were worth very little.

**Every review chain was grown, none was planned.** The lowest permanent key on
any review or integration task sits above the entire scaffolded range. Nobody
scheduled review at the start; every chain exists because a session decided,
mid-run, that its own work needed an adversarial read.

**Review is a function of session kind, and the loop reviewed down its own value
curve.** 42% of design sessions earned a review against 13% of modelling
sessions ([`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md) §*Why the curve cannot be read*),
and 24% of producers overall ([`loop-record.md`](loop-record.md) §*The tree at a
glance*). Findings per review fell from a mean of 6.2
over the first five chains to 3.5 over the last four — **and that is not decay**.
The first five read first-of-their-kind artifacts; the last four read incremental
decisions on artifacts already reviewed two to five times. **The falling count is
a selection effect of a rising review rate, and every review at the margin still
found something.**

**The yield question is answered with a null, and the null is honest.** Nine
chains cannot separate flat from falling: the gap is ≈1.8σ before any confound,
reaching 3σ would need roughly 25 chains of matched subject size, and — the deeper
problem — **the unit is elastic.** Sub-corrections per finding run from 1.0 to
3.3, and consequence is not captured at all: two findings that reversed whole
verdicts count the same as one corrected transition count. **More chains is
therefore the weaker fix; a severity scale fixed before the reviewer starts is
the prior one**, because without a fixed unit a longer run measures more
enumeration style rather than more review.

**What review chains actually bought here was the handoff, not the head count.**
The campaign's most-quoted anecdote — three readers of one artifact, each finding
what the two before had not — survives only in a much narrower form
([`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md) §6). It is **one** node, and it is
the node the loop selected *because it was hardest*: selection, not sampling. And
the three readers were **not independent — they were a relay.** Each session
wrote its specific doubt into the next session's task file, and the next
session's sharpest finding is that doubt, answered. The findings nobody aimed at
came from an **instrument**, not from a reader: they came out of the claim-library
sweep of Part 3 §3. **A chain of three unaimed readers is a different experiment,
and this workstream did not run it.**

**Spending an in-session reviewer changed the session that spent it, three times
out of three** ([`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md) §*The in-session channel,
and the five leaves that declined it*).
One reviewer **broke a disposition its session had already landed**, forcing a
reversal across nine artifacts. One swept the full claim library and found two
violations, one of which nobody was looking for. One made its session withdraw a
sentence it had already written. **None of the three was a second opinion; all
three were a different instrument** — which is the useful way to decide whether to
spend one.

**And declining a reviewer is a real answer.** Five sessions considered the
allowance and did not spend it, for reasons that are not the same reason. One
spent four executable tests instead, arguing that a test which breaks if the
central claim is wrong beats a fresh context reading the same prose. One declined
*and* cut no review task, because a later task was already chartered to contest
its verdict — **"a review leaf here would buy the same read twice."** Two could
not spend it because the harness forbade subagents, and cut a full review chain
instead, which one of them calls the stronger instrument.

**The loop's commit discipline is what made any of this measurable.**
[`review-yield.md`](review-yield.md) §*Five of nine dispositions are not in
`.grove/` at all* — `.grove/` being the directory the task tree lives in: five
integrations wrote nothing back into their own task file
and recorded their per-finding dispositions **in the commit message** instead.
Since the task tree is process state that gets deleted at teardown while commits
are not, the commit message turned out to be the *more* durable record. **One
task, one focused commit, held across all 73 tasks — no task in this workstream
carries more than one commit.** That rule was written for tidiness; what it
bought was the ability to measure the loop four days later.

**A recording obligation with no instrument behind it decays into whatever the
last entry looked like.** The modelling phase pre-registered that every material
finding would carry three tags, recorded before the fix. Two of the eight
measures ended up kept as specified; five were kept unevenly, and one whole
column of findings carries no tags at all
([`formalism-findings.md`](formalism-findings.md) §*What the ledger supports*).
Nothing detected it — the runner checks obligations, not ledger entries — and
**it decayed at exactly the boundary where one session handed off to the next.**
This is the strongest argument in the whole record for the thing Part 2's rule A′
is about: a discipline nobody can fail is a discipline nobody keeps.

**And the campaign's own verdict on its two tools is that neither was the most
productive instrument in it.** That was **the pair `(claim, the control that
would falsify it)`, read adversarially by a fresh context**
([`formalism-findings.md`](formalism-findings.md) §*Cost, counterfactual,
verdict*). One review session found six real defects in a suite that was green,
coverage-asserted, and had already published its own list of things it might be
wrong about — and **four of the six were invisible to any run of that suite.**

## What this account does not establish

- **One workstream, one subject, one operator.** Every measurement here comes
  from formal-modelling work in a repository whose entire purpose was rigour
  about evidence. That is close to the least likely place in the world to observe
  review yield decaying, or a session shrugging at a false green. **The hit rates
  are hit rates here.**
- **It counts sessions, findings and commits — never effort or money.** There is
  no token count anywhere in the record, and the phrase *not worth the money* in
  Part 1 is a judgement about outcomes, not a division.
- **The session figures are one pin behind the tree.** They are derived at change
  `wzmyysov`; four sessions ran after it.
- **It cannot see a finding nobody wrote down.** A review that noticed something
  and judged it not worth acting on leaves no trace — by design. **This
  document's floor is firmer than its ceiling.**
- **It adjudicates the six claims the campaign made about itself, not the
  campaign.** Lessons nobody wrote down at the time are outside its scope by
  construction, and there is no instrument that would have caught them.
- **Nothing here was re-measured.** [`candidate-lessons.md`](candidate-lessons.md)
  re-opened every model file it cites; this document re-opened that document and
  the three beside it. No model was re-run and no figure above is new. What
  replaces an instrument is the citation — and that bar exists because this tree
  failed it twice before anyone was watching for it: two citations named a test
  file for what its *filename* suggested rather than for what it *asserts*.
